Run the grid

At 12:33 on 28 April 2025, Spain and Portugal went dark in seconds. A power grid has to match supply and demand every second, and hold its in a narrow band while it does it. Can you keep the lights on?

The blackout replay

28 April 2025: ninety seconds, in slow motion

It was not a lack of power. Spain was exporting about 4.3 GW to its neighbours at 12:30. It was voltage: it climbed across the grid, plants disconnected to protect themselves, and every disconnection pushed voltage higher. Only at the end did frequency collapse. Every value below comes from the official reports, tagged with its source.

The grid at 12:30

A sunny Monday with very cheap power: the day‑ahead price averaged €18.50/MWh and was zero or negative in the middle hours GOV. Solar supplied more than half of the electricity. Pumped‑storage plants were using the surplus to pump water uphill.

The morning: oscillations, and a cure with a side effect

Two bouts of shook the grid around noon. The operator, Red Eléctrica (REE), damped them with standard tools: more lines in service, lower exports, a fixed setting on the link to France. Every report agrees on the side effect: those measures raised voltages.

    Who's to blame? The reports agree on the physics, not the fault

    Every official report calls it a multifactorial overvoltage: not enough dynamic voltage control, oscillation fixes that raised voltages, and disconnections, some of them improper. Who should have absorbed more is still disputed. Here are the positions side by side. This page doesn't pick a winner.

    ENTSO-E Expert Panel final report, 20 Mar 2026

    • The key phenomenon was that voltage control in Spain was not effective. Renewable plants ran at a , so as their output fell, so did the reactive power they absorbed. FINAL
    • Several large synchronous generators reached their reactive‑power reference in less than 75% of hourly samples. Shunt reactors were switched by hand. Some protection settings were below the requirements. FINAL
    • Simulations: increased reactive power margins could have prevented system collapse. The report says it does not assign liability. FINAL

    Spanish government committee 17 Jun 2025

    • Not enough dynamic voltage control: units either weren't scheduled, didn't deliver what the rules required, or both. GOV
    • REE scheduled 10 thermal units for voltage control; one south‑west unit dropped out the evening before and was not replaced. GOV
    • Some disconnections were apparently improper. No evidence of a cyberattack. GOV

    Red Eléctrica (REE) grid operator

    • Generators with voltage‑control duties under the operating rule failed to comply with them. REE
    • Its scheduling was sufficient; inertia was above ENTSO-E's recommendation. REE
    • On the final report: Red Eléctrica no falló; REE says the panel does not name its scheduling as a cause. REE 2026

    The utilities (AELEC) Iberdrola, Endesa, EDP

    • REE alone was responsible: it chose to manage voltage with limited synchronous capacity, unevenly spread across the country. AELEC
    • Their plants met the rules. Voltage swings fell 37–52% in the days after, once more gas plants ran. AELEC
    • Endesa's CEO: the central cause was REE scheduling un número reducido de grupos able to control voltage dynamically. El Español

    The final report adds a third party to the story: the rules themselves. Fixed power factor for renewables, a 435 kV operating limit with little margin below generators' 435–440 kV ride‑through limits, and no economic penalty for missing reactive targets. FINAL The regulator, CNMC, has since let renewables provide voltage control (June 2025) and added a simpler fixed‑setpoint mode (July 2026). CNMC

    Method

    How this page works

    The replay is the record, not a simulation

    Every time, value and place in the replay comes from the official reports: millisecond timestamps from the Spanish government committee (GOV), causes and the generation‑loss totals from the ENTSO-E Expert Panel (FACTUAL and FINAL). Where they disagree, the event card says so. The chart joins reported frequency values with straight lines; it is not a measured trace. Voltage dots are values at different substations and are not joined.

    The numbers at 12:30 are REE's own data

    Demand and the generation mix come from REE's public 5‑minute data for peninsular Spain, retrieved on 16 October 2026 and embedded in this page. REE's 12:30 demand, 25,184 MW, matches the government report exactly. REE's hydro figure is net of pumping, so conventional hydro is shown as hydro plus pumping, which reproduces the government report's shares (solar 54%, nuclear 10%, gas 3%). "Other" is coal, cogeneration, biomass and the small remainder REE does not break out.
