The grid in six ideas

Everything on this page rests on six ideas. None needs any maths. Each one has a small thing to tap or drag.

Idea 1

Supply must match demand, every second

The wires store almost nothing. Every second, power stations must produce exactly what homes, offices and factories use. On a normal spring day, peninsular Spain used between 20 and 32 GW (a gigawatt is 1,000 megawatts, about what one large power station makes). REE data

Generation25.0 GW
Idea 2

Frequency is the grid's heartbeat

Alternating current swings back and forth 50 times a second, and every big generator spins in step with it. Use more power than is produced and the generators slow down, so frequency falls. Produce too much and it rises. Normal is within ±0.2 Hz of 50. ENTSO-E

50.00 Hz
Idea 3

Inertia: the grid's flywheel

Spinning generators (nuclear, gas, hydro) store energy in their heavy rotors. When a plant suddenly trips, that stored energy slows the fall in frequency and buys seconds for reserves to react. Solar panels and most wind turbines connect through electronics and don't add any.

Same loss, two real moments from REE's data.
Idea 4

Voltage is pressure, and it's local

Frequency is one number for the whole grid. Voltage differs from place to place, like water pressure in different streets. It is pushed up or down by : injecting it raises nearby voltage, absorbing it lowers it. Spain's main grid must stay between 380 and 435 kV. GOV

Town Babsorb ← → inject
Idea 5

Protection: plants disconnect to save themselves

Every plant and line has relays that disconnect it if voltage or frequency goes beyond set limits, to avoid damage. Plants must ride through disturbances up to those limits. The danger is a chain: if one disconnection makes things worse, the next plant goes, then the next. That's a cascade.

Idea 6

Spain is wired into Europe

Spain and Portugal are part of one synchronous grid with continental Europe, running at the same 50 Hz, through AC lines and a to France, plus a link to Morocco. GOV Europe's size steadies them. If the links are cut, Iberia becomes an island on its own. That happened at 12:33:21 on 28 April.

The lesson

Same loss, different grid: what inertia buys you

When a power plant trips, the first thing that happens is that every spinning machine on the grid slows a little, handing over some of its stored energy. The more energy is spinning, the slower falls, and the more time automatic reserves have to catch it. Pick two real moments from REE's data and drop the same plant out of both.

Grid A

Grid B

Two things this does not mean. Inertia sets how fast frequency falls, not whether it recovers: that is the reserves' job. And it was not what failed on 28 April. The cascade began with frequency at about 50 Hz, and the final report's simulations found that even with significantly higher inertia values, the loss of system synchronism would not have been avoided. The blackout replay

28 April 2025: ninety seconds, in slow motion

It was not a lack of power. Spain was exporting about 4.3 GW to its neighbours at 12:30. It was voltage: it climbed across the grid, plants disconnected to protect themselves, and every disconnection pushed voltage higher. Only at the end did frequency collapse. Every value below comes from the official reports, tagged with its source.

The grid at 12:30

A sunny Monday with very cheap power: the day‑ahead price averaged €18.50/MWh and was zero or negative in the middle hours GOV. Solar supplied more than half of the electricity. Pumped‑storage plants were using the surplus to pump water uphill.

The morning: oscillations, and a cure with a side effect

Two bouts of shook the grid around noon. The operator, Red Eléctrica (REE), damped them with standard tools: more lines in service, lower exports, a fixed setting on the link to France. Every report agrees on the side effect: those measures raised voltages.

      Bringing a country back from zero

      A dead grid can't simply be switched back on: power stations need power to start. Operators built small islands of supply and grew them. Islands around the lines from France (Catalonia, the Basque Country) and Morocco (the south), and hydro plants in the Duero, Galicia and Asturias‑Cantabria. Then they joined the islands together. Some attempts failed and had to be repeated. GOVFINAL

        Myths vs evidence

        What didn't cause it, and what's half true

        A cyberattack no evidence

        The government ran what the minister called the largest cybersecurity investigation in the country's history. It found no evidence of cyber-incident or cyber-attack, though it did find vulnerabilities. GOV

        A rare "atmospheric vibration" false

        Portugal's grid operator REN denied ever issuing the statement. The weather agency AEMET found no unusual phenomena. EDMO traced the claim to a deleted press alert. REN · AEMET · EDMO

        Too much solar half true

        ENTSO-E's president: It's not about renewables, it's about voltage control. But most units that tripped were inverter‑based, and the fixed power‑factor rule made renewables amplify the voltage rise. It was the rules and settings, not the solar share: on 22 April solar was 51% at 12:30, and nothing happened. POWERFINALMaldita

        Low inertia not the cause

        Inertia was above ENTSO-E's recommendation, and the cascade started with frequency at about 50 Hz. The final report's simulations: higher inertia alone would not have avoided the loss of synchronism. REEFINAL

        More nuclear would have saved it not automatically

        3,078.6 MW of nuclear was declared unavailable (refuelling or maintenance); four reactors were connected. Synchronous units, nuclear included, were among those that didn't meet their reactive reference. Spinning machines only help voltage if they absorb reactive power. GOVFINAL

        Three readings

        Who's to blame? The reports agree on the physics, not the fault

        Every official report calls it a multifactorial overvoltage: not enough dynamic voltage control, oscillation fixes that raised voltages, and disconnections, some of them improper. Who should have absorbed more is still disputed. Here are the positions side by side. This page doesn't pick a winner.

        ENTSO-E Expert Panel final report, 20 Mar 2026

        • The key phenomenon was that voltage control in Spain was not effective. Renewable plants ran at a , so as their output fell, so did the reactive power they absorbed. FINAL
        • Several large synchronous generators reached their reactive‑power reference in less than 75% of hourly samples. Shunt reactors were switched by hand. Some protection settings were below the requirements. FINAL
        • Simulations: increased reactive power margins could have prevented system collapse. The report says it does not assign liability. FINAL

        Spanish government committee 17 Jun 2025

        • Not enough dynamic voltage control: units either weren't scheduled, didn't deliver what the rules required, or both. GOV
        • REE scheduled 10 thermal units for voltage control; one south‑west unit dropped out the evening before and was not replaced. GOV
        • Some disconnections were apparently improper. No evidence of a cyberattack. GOV

        Red Eléctrica (REE) grid operator

        • Generators with voltage‑control duties under the operating rule failed to comply with them. REE
        • Its scheduling was sufficient; inertia was above ENTSO-E's recommendation. REE
        • On the final report: Red Eléctrica no falló; REE says the panel does not name its scheduling as a cause. REE 2026

        The utilities (AELEC) Iberdrola, Endesa, EDP

        • REE alone was responsible: it chose to manage voltage with limited synchronous capacity, unevenly spread across the country. AELEC
        • Their plants met the rules. Voltage swings fell 37–52% in the days after, once more gas plants ran. AELEC
        • Endesa's CEO: the central cause was REE scheduling un número reducido de grupos able to control voltage dynamically. El Español

        The final report adds a third party to the story: the rules themselves. Fixed power factor for renewables, a 435 kV operating limit with little margin below generators' 435–440 kV ride‑through limits, and no economic penalty for missing reactive targets. FINAL The regulator, CNMC, has since let renewables provide voltage control (June 2025) and added a simpler fixed‑setpoint mode (July 2026). CNMC

        Level 1: the frequency model

        Frequency follows the aggregated : a mismatch between generation and load speeds up or slows down every synchronous machine at once, and the stored spinning energy sets how fast.

        df/dt = (Pgen − Pload) · f0 / (2 · Ekin)    Ekin = Σ Hi · Si

        f0 is 50 Hz, P is in MW and Ekin in MW·s. Only synchronous machines count towards Ekin: nuclear, gas, coal and cogeneration, solar thermal, hydro and pumped storage, in both pumping and generating mode. Solar PV, wind and imports add none. Each step of the game also adds automatic reserves, load that falls slightly with frequency, and the protection steps listed under the game.

        The voltage panel and the what‑if toy

        One aggregated 400 kV node: voltage = 400 kV + a sensitivity × (reactive power injected − absorbed). Shunt reactors absorb Q = B·V². Synchronous plants absorb in proportion to voltage above a 410 kV dead band (the 405–410 kV band with no obligation, ARXIV's reading of REE), at half strength "as on 28 April". Renewables absorb a fixed share of their output at fixed power factor, or follow voltage in voltage‑control mode. The Granada plant's 165 Mvar is from the final report. Every other size is illustrative, chosen so that the default sits at 417 kV (GOV: 410–420 kV at 12:30).

        The what‑if toy adds six groups of renewable plants named after the reported trips, each with an overvoltage trip setting, and a slow rise standing in for the scheduled output cuts and small losses before 12:33. Its baseline trips Granada at about 52 seconds and runs away at about 70 (real: 57 and 76). It was tuned so its levers behave as the reports reason. It cannot confirm or refute any lever. The toy's verdict is shown apart from the reports' own words.
