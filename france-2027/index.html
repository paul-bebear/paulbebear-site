01 · The baseline

Where France stands under Macron

Every promise below is a change from something. That something is a country with a hung parliament, a 5% deficit, rising unemployment and lenders who are starting to charge more.

Politics

The July 2024 snap election left no majority. Michel Barnier was censured in December 2024 and François Bayrou lost a confidence vote in September 2025. Sébastien Lecornu has been prime minister since then. He got the 2026 budget through only with article 49.3, the procedure that passes a bill without a vote, and won Socialist abstention by suspending the pension reform.

On 1 October 2026 he presented the 2027 budget: €54bn of savings, a deficit target of 5.0% of GDP, housing benefit (APL) frozen, a new tax on jet-fuel suppliers and fees for BTS and prépa students who don't hold a grant. Parliament has until about 26 November.

Economy

Unemployment reached 8.3% in Q2 2026, up 0.7 points in a year, and youth unemployment jumped 2.5 points. Inflation was 3.1% in September, almost entirely energy (+21.3% year on year); core inflation was 1.5%.

The 10-year bond rate passed 4.8% in early October, for the first time since 2008, and the spread over German debt hit about 120 basis points at the end of September, the widest since the euro crisis. One Italian market report put France's 10-year above Italy's on 6 October. That matters for both programmes, because both are partly financed on credit.

02 · The race

Who is actually running

Macron can't stand again. The RN leads every poll; the open question is who meets it in the run-off on 2 May.

Jean-Luc Mélenchon, LFI

Declared May 2026 · fourth run · 2012: 11.1% · 2017: 19.6% · 2022: 22.0%

  • Programme: L'Avenir en commun, 831 measures in its January 2025 edition. A 2027 edition is due on 6 November 2026.
  • The left is split. Raphaël Glucksmann won the Socialist and Place publique primary on 11 October with 66%, and the Communist Fabien Roussel is running too. Mélenchon's case is that he is the only left candidate who can reach the run-off.
  • The 2024 New Popular Front (NFP) programme, written with the Socialists, Greens and Communists, overlaps heavily with LFI's, which is why the NFP costings are used below.

Marine Le Pen, RN, with Jordan Bardella

Declared 7 July 2026 · fourth run · Bardella named as her prime minister

  • The court case. She was convicted in March 2025 of embezzling European Parliament funds and banned from office with immediate effect. On 7 July 2026 the Paris appeal court upheld the conviction but cut the ban to 45 months, 30 of them suspended. Commentators read that as leaving her eligible. She has still appealed to the Cour de cassation, which suspends the sentence and has said it will rule before the first round. If she is barred, Bardella runs.
  • The Bardella story. In late September Mediapart published messages it says Bardella wrote in 2013–15, including antisemitic remarks about Jews and banks. He denies writing them and is suing; Mediapart stands by its authentication.
  • Programme: a costed "counter-budget" presented on 6 October 2026, plus the 2024 referendum bill on immigration. Pension details are still to come.

04 · Money

Who pays, and does it add up?

Both oppositions say their programme pays for itself. Every independent costing finds a gap. The size of the gap is the argument.

The spending gap

In June 2024 the NFP said its programme would cost €150bn a year by end-2027, matched by €150bn of new revenue, mostly from taxes on the rich: about €15bn from a restored wealth tax and €15bn from a windfall-profits tax. The Institut Montaigne put the net effect at €179bn a year added to the deficit. The liberal iFRAP found only about €55bn of realistic revenue, against roughly €215bn of spending, with retirement at 60 alone at about €50bn by 2027. For Mélenchon's 2022 programme the numbers were €250bn of spending, €267bn of revenue by his count, and a €219bn deficit by Montaigne's.

The RN's 6 October 2026 counter-budget claims the opposite: €141.7bn of net savings by 2031 and a deficit of 3.7% as early as 2027, while cutting taxes by €53bn in year one. The Institut de l'Entreprise went through its 125 measures line by line and found about €7.5bn it considers credible. It judged 80% of the financing undocumented, legally fragile or without effect on the deficit. In 2024 Montaigne had put the RN's legislative programme at €71bn a year of extra deficit.

Does the demand-side logic hold?

LFI's reasoning: give low and middle earners more, and they spend most of it; invest publicly, and you create jobs; tax revenue follows growth. That is standard Keynesian economics, and it is reasonable when there is slack and room to borrow. France in 2026 has one but not the other.

  • Inflation is not the binding risk. With 8.3% unemployment and core inflation at 1.5%, a demand boost would push prices up somewhat, mainly in services, but not on the scale of 2022. Today's 3.1% headline is an energy shock that wage rises would not cause.
  • Borrowing costs are the binding risk. The deficit is 5.4% and the 10-year rate is near 4.8%. Each 0.1-point rise in that rate costs about €0.3bn in the first year and €3.2bn after nine years, as the debt rolls over. A programme whose revenue arrives later than its spending is betting that lenders believe the revenue will come.
  • Spending leaks abroad. France imports a large share of what households buy, so part of any boost becomes a trade deficit rather than French jobs. That is what happened in 1981–83, when Mitterrand raised the SMIC 10%, introduced retirement at 60 and nationalised banks: three devaluations later came the 1983 U-turn to austerity. Inside the euro there is no devaluation; the pressure would show up in bond spreads instead.
  • The ECB backstop has conditions. The ECB's tool for stopping a spread spiral (the TPI) requires a country to respect EU fiscal rules. LFI plans to disobey those rules.
  • The SMIC is already high. France's minimum wage is among the highest in the OECD relative to the median wage, around 60%. Studies of past French increases found real job losses for low-skilled workers (Kramarz and Philippon, 2001). Montaigne costed the €1,600 SMIC at €3.5bn a year to the state, because employer contributions near the SMIC are subsidised.

Verdict: the direction is defensible; the scale and timing are the problem. Spending on the 2024 scale only works if the wealth taxes yield close to what is booked, and the evidence below suggests they would yield less.

Tax avoidance: what the studies say, including below the billionaires

You don't have to rely on the billionaire study alone. There is evidence across the whole top of the distribution:

  • IPP, "Which taxes do billionaires pay?" (2023). Using 2016 tax data, the overall tax rate climbs to about 46% within the top 0.1% of households, then falls to 26% for the richest 0.0002% (about 75 households). The cause is income kept inside holding companies, taxed only at corporate rates. The decline starts within the top 0.1% and 0.01%, which is roughly the €10–100M band. The method is disputed: counting undistributed profits as household income lowers the measured rate, and the data are ten years old.
  • The 2026 holding-company tax is aimed at exactly that channel. The Socialists' compromise of a 3% minimum tax from €10M, sparing family and innovative businesses, was rejected in October 2025.
  • Migration. In Sweden and Denmark (Jakobsen, Kleven, Kolsrud, Landais and Muñoz), each extra point of top wealth tax reduces the number of wealthy taxpayers by about 2% over time, with small effects on jobs and investment overall.
  • Avoidance over flight. Swiss evidence (Brülhart and co-authors) finds declared wealth reacts strongly to wealth tax, mostly through reporting and avoidance rather than people moving.
  • France's own record. The official evaluation of the 2018 reform counted about 950 departures against 370 returns a year among wealth-tax payers in 2011–16. In 2020, under the property-only tax, it counted 220 departures against 380 returns. It found little measurable effect on investment from abolishing the ISF.
  • Norway saw a wave of wealthy emigrants after raising its wealth tax in 2022. How much it cost is still disputed.

Gabriel Zucman puts his 2% tax at about €20bn a year from some 1,800 households; opponents argue avoidance and departures would cut that sharply. The evidence suggests a wealth tax raises real money but less than its static estimate, and that design (no holding-company loopholes, an exit tax) matters more than the rate.

Pressure test · RN

The RN's money has the mirror problem

The RN's tax cuts are certain and immediate: €12.2bn for VAT on energy (more for households that heat big homes or drive far, and dearer when oil rises), €17.1bn for local business taxes. Its savings are the uncertain part. It expects €29bn from immigration by 2031, 92% of which the Institut de l'Entreprise rates as undocumented or legally fragile. It expects €13.1bn from cutting the EU contribution, which needs every member state to agree. Collecting VAT at source is booked at €18bn, one and a half times the €12bn the Commission estimates escapes VAT in France altogether. On the institute's reading, the deficit would be 5.9% in 2027, not 3.7%.

05 · Immigration

More rights, or national priority

LFI would widen legal status and rights; the RN would tie rights to nationality and shut most entry routes. Both collide with a constraint: the Constitution and European law for the RN, administrative capacity and housing for LFI.

Scale today

7.3M immigrants, 10.7% of the population (INSEE, 2023). 4.5M foreigners hold a valid residence permit, 8.1% of residents (end 2025).

Flows, 2025

384k first permits (+11%). Humanitarian permits +65%. 116k asylum claims. 28.6k regularisations (−10%).

Enforcement

About 25k removals (+15.7%), most of them forced. The EU Pact adds border screening from June 2026.

Pressure test · LFI

Guardrails: what the LFI programme says and doesn't say

What it covers: legal status, rights and fighting exploitation. Regularising people already working and paying in is the strongest part. Spain's 2005 amnesty of about 570,000 people raised social-security receipts, according to Monras, Vázquez-Grenno and Elias (2018).

What it doesn't: there is no target number, no removals policy, nothing on vetting beyond keeping the asylum office (OFPRA) independent, and no plan for the "pull" question, on which the evidence from repeated Italian and Spanish amnesties is mixed. Suspending the Dublin rules and "leaving the dead end of Schengen and Frontex" would put France at odds with the EU Pact that has just come into force. Capacity is the practical limit: asylum housing, prefecture backlogs and language courses are already stretched under today's flows.

Pressure test · RN

Legal walls

"National priority" for jobs, housing and benefits breaks the constitutional principle of equality, the European Convention on Human Rights (non-discrimination and family life) and the EU directive on long-term residents (2003/109). Hence the referendum. But changing the Constitution by direct referendum under article 11 rather than the article 89 route is itself contested; de Gaulle did it once, in 1962. Even then, EU law and the Convention would still apply unless France left them. Ending family reunification runs into the same right to family life.

The housing link, and the case for building

More legal residents without more homes means higher rents, and LFI's rent caps don't solve that. San Francisco's rent control cut the supply of rentals by about 15% as landlords converted units (Diamond, McQuade and Qian, 2019). LFI's answer is 200,000 public homes a year for five years, a million in total, which would be among the largest building programmes since the 1970s.

The idea of pairing immigration with new towns has a French precedent: the villes nouvelles (Cergy-Pontoise, Évry, Marne-la-Vallée, Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines, Sénart and others) were planned from 1965, built from the late 1960s with a building trade that relied heavily on immigrant labour, and now house around a million people. Construction still relies heavily on non-EU workers today, which is also why the RN's €1,000-a-year hiring levy would hit building costs. The sticking point is timing: arrivals are immediate, while a new town takes ten to twenty years. Neither programme plans the two together.

06 · Policing

What "community policing" means here

LFI: local, but not decentralised

The model is the police de proximité that Lionel Jospin's government ran from 1998 until Nicolas Sarkozy wound it down in 2003: officers assigned to a neighbourhood, on foot, known to residents, focused on prevention and taking complaints.

LFI would fold municipal police into this national force after a few months' training, deploy it by population and need, and put it under the functional authority of the mayor but the hierarchical authority of the prefect, the state's representative. Add ID-check receipts, an end to arrest quotas, a ban on facial recognition, and doubled staff for financial crime and trafficking.

RN: more force, more deterrence

Mandatory minimum sentences, a legal presumption of self-defence for officers, more prison places, and expulsion of foreign offenders. The RN frames crime partly as an immigration issue, which connects this to its immigration and benefits plans.

On drugs, the RN opposes legalisation. LFI would create a state monopoly on cannabis, with tax revenue earmarked for addiction prevention. Canada (2018) and Uruguay (2013) show legal markets work as markets; how much of the illegal trade they displace depends on price and access.

Pressure test · LFI

Does local policing degenerate into local fiefdoms?

The design has the guardrail you'd want: officers stay in a national force with national training, pay and discipline, and the prefect, not the mayor, controls careers. That is the opposite of a local militia. The real risk is different. Disbanding the BAC and BRAV-M removes the units used against organised drug gangs and violent riots, and the programme doesn't say what replaces them. The 1998–2003 experiment was judged good for trust and poor value for money, and short of staff for night work.

07 · Energy

One side wants no nuclear, the other no wind

France gets about two-thirds of its electricity from nuclear. Today's plan builds six EPR2 reactors and keeps the old ones running. Each opposition drops one half of the low-carbon mix.

The grid operator RTE's 2021 study Futurs énergétiques 2050 modelled six paths to carbon neutrality. A 100% renewable path (M0) was technically possible but needed the fastest build-out of solar and wind in Europe, plus large storage, and was the most expensive: on Montaigne's reading of RTE's figures, about €18bn a year more than the cheapest, roughly half-nuclear path (N03). That is LFI's problem.

The RN's problem is the reverse. Its moratorium stops new wind and solar now, while no new EPR is expected before about 2038. Carbone 4 says the strategy "doesn't hold" for that reason: for a decade, demand from electric cars, heat pumps and industry would grow with no new supply. Both oppositions also promise lower bills: LFI through regulated prices and nationalisation, the RN through a VAT cut and a "national price".

Pressure test · LFI

Is the nuclear exit a play for voters from former test sites?

France carried out 17 nuclear tests in the Algerian Sahara (1960–66) and 193 in French Polynesia (1966–96), and the damage is real. LFI's programme does call for compensating test victims, in Polynesia especially. But there's no sign the energy policy is aimed at those voters. The anti-nuclear stance comes from LFI's ecologist turn: it ran a "citizens' vote" on the nuclear exit in 2018, and in 2012 Mélenchon promised only a referendum because his Communist allies were pro-nuclear. Where LFI's appeal to voters of North African and African origin is real (Ifop estimated 69% of Muslim voters backed Mélenchon in the 2022 first round), it rests on Gaza, discrimination and policing, not energy.

08 · NATO & Europe

What leaving NATO would actually mean

LFI's position is two-step: leave the integrated command immediately, as de Gaulle did in 1966 (France returned in 2009), then leave the alliance itself in stages. Article 13 of the treaty lets a member leave one year after giving notice. The January 2026 resolution came while Washington was threatening Greenland, a NATO ally's territory, which made "an alliance led by a power outside international law" an easier argument to make.

  • The nuclear deterrent is unaffected. France never put its nuclear forces under NATO planning.
  • Europe. 23 of the 27 EU members are in NATO. Poland, the Baltic states and the Nordics see it as their life insurance against Russia, and would read a French exit as abandonment. France would lose influence over the European defence it says it wants. The EU's own mutual-assistance clause (article 42.7) remains, but LFI also wants out of EU defence programmes, so France would stand outside both frameworks.
  • The United States would lose a partner for intelligence, logistics and its African and Middle Eastern operations, and could retaliate on trade. LFI's resolution explicitly plans for American reprisals.
  • China and Russia both gain from a divided West, which is the core objection. LFI's answer is a non-aligned role like India's. India, though, has no hostile neighbour already at war with its partners.

The RN's position has moved the other way. Le Pen promised in 2022 to leave the integrated command; Bardella said in 2024 that treaties shouldn't change in wartime. The RN's past ties to Moscow, including a €9.4M loan from a Russian-linked bank in 2014 (repaid in 2023), remain its weak point on foreign policy.

Pressure test · both

The EU budget and the EU rules

Both oppositions would confront Brussels: the RN by cutting France's €31bn contribution (it has no legal way to do so alone), LFI by ignoring deficit and competition rules. The difference is the target. The RN wants less Europe in general; LFI wants a different Europe, and keeps the euro.

09 · The constitution

A Sixth Republic, and recalling officials

LFI's process

  1. An article 11 referendum asks voters to start a constituent process and sets how the assembly is chosen: voting system, gender parity, citizens drawn by lot.
  2. No sitting or former member of parliament may join it, and its members can't stand in the next elections.
  3. After two years the draft goes to a referendum. If voters say no, the assembly starts again.

The aim is a stable parliamentary system: proportional representation, no 49.3, a weaker president, and a citizens' initiative referendum (RIC) that can recall officials, propose or repeal laws and amend the constitution.

What the precedents say

Constituent assemblies are slow and often fail. Chile rejected two drafts by referendum, in 2022 and 2023. Iceland's crowdsourced constitution of 2011 was never adopted. Two years of institutional limbo would overlap with the budget and bond-market pressures above.

Recall works where thresholds are high. California recalled a governor in 2003; Taiwan's 2025 mass recall of legislators failed at every vote. A low threshold produces permanent campaigning, while a high one makes recall rare. LFI hasn't published its threshold. The RN wants more referendums too, so recall is the one institutional idea the two share.

10 · Rights

Discrimination, religion and the antisemitism charge

LFI's programme combines a national anti-discrimination plan (systematic testing, anonymous applications, ID-check receipts) with an interministerial plan against islamophobia and antisemitism, protection for mosques and synagogues, and repeal of the separatism law, which it says targets Muslims. The RN's 2022 programme went the other way: a public-space headscarf ban and a law against "Islamist ideologies".

The antisemitism charge against LFI rests on specific acts: refusing to call Hamas a terrorist organisation after 7 October 2023, staying away from the November 2023 march against antisemitism, and Mélenchon calling antisemitism "residual" in 2024. In an Ipsos survey for the Crif (2024), 55% of LFI supporters agreed with at least six of 16 antisemitic statements, against 52% of RN supporters and 46% of all French adults. 92% of Jewish respondents in another survey said LFI fuels antisemitism, against 49% for the RN. LFI rejects the charge and points to its programme.

The RN's version is older: its founder Jean-Marie Le Pen was repeatedly convicted for remarks about the Holocaust, and Marine Le Pen expelled him in 2015. The Mediapart allegations about Bardella's 2013–15 messages, which he denies, revived it this autumn. Polls suggest neither party has paid much electoral price.

11 · Scorecard

Strongest and weakest points

Not a recommendation. Where each programme's own case is most solid, and where it is most exposed.

Macron baseline

Strongest: the nuclear relaunch; a modest minimum tax on top incomes and holdings; continuity with EU and NATO partners.

Weakest: it doesn't fix the deficit either. Debt rose from about €2.26 trillion in 2017 to over €3.5 trillion, unemployment is rising, and the pension reform is suspended.

LFI

Strongest: tax progressivity (the 14-bracket schedule cuts tax for most earners); regularising people who already work here; the renewable build-out; neighbourhood policing with national guardrails.

Weakest: a spending gap of €100bn+ at a time when lenders charge 4.8%; the nuclear exit; leaving NATO; disobeying the EU rules that make the ECB backstop available; no numbers or guardrails on immigration.

RN

Strongest: the nuclear commitment; costed tax cuts for workers under 30 and for families; a fiscal "golden rule" in principle.

Weakest: financing that rests on immigration and EU savings it can't legally obtain; national priority against the Constitution and the ECHR; the wind and solar moratorium; a candidate still before the Cour de cassation.
