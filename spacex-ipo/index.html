SpaceX's IPO, without the spin

A viral video says your retirement account is about to be force-fed an unprofitable $1.75T company. Most of it is true. The one mechanism it blames is described backwards. Here's the honest version — then dig into the Full Teardown for receipts.

The one-paragraph version

SpaceX is going public at ~$1.75 trillion while losing ~$4.9B a year. New Nasdaq rules let it enter the Nasdaq-100 just ~15 trading days after listing, which forces every index fund to buy it — and since those funds are fully invested, they pay for it by selling a slice of everything they already own. The video gets that real mechanism right. Where it's wrong: it calls the "3× weighting rule" a demand amplifier, when it's actually a cap that limits how much of the stock funds must hold. The genuine catch is structural — if you own an index fund, you'll own SpaceX and can't opt out.

What SpaceX actually is

Three businesses in a trench coat: Starlink (the real engine — $11.4B revenue, 63% margins, cash-generative), a rocket business (~$4B, fine but small), and xAI (the Grok/X AI lab it bought for $250B, burning ~$1B a month). Net result: an $18.7B-revenue company that still lost $4.9B.

What the video gets right Verified

Index funds will be forced to buy SpaceX the moment it joins the Nasdaq-100, at whatever price the market sets — because that's how passive investing works. The rules were changed to make this happen fast (15 days vs. up to a year) and to let a tiny-float company qualify at all. Insiders sell into that forced demand later. "Your 401(k) is the exit liquidity" is a fair description of the mechanism.

What it gets wrong Inverted

It frames the "3× multiplier" as manufacturing demand. The rule actually caps a low-float stock's index weight below its full market value — it's a brake, not an accelerator. The video also understates xAI's revenue and uses a stale Anthropic valuation. The thesis survives; the dramatized details don't.

The simulation, in one line

Only ~$75–87B of stock is actually tradable, yet forced index buying of roughly $7B (conservative) to $22–27B chases it — that's 10–35% of the entire float, price-insensitive, in days. The money is pulled out of the existing Nasdaq-100 — overwhelmingly the giants (Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet) — and rotated into SpaceX. Every incumbent gets the same % trim; the small names are dwarfed, not spared. And it's not one event — SpaceX, OpenAI and Anthropic all list within ~5 months, stacking three rotations into one half-year (Section 08).

What you're actually buying

Class A shares carry one vote; Musk's Class B carry ten. He keeps ~85% of the vote on ~42% of the equity, claims "controlled company" status to skip independent-board rules, and — via a mandatory-arbitration clause — leaves holders with no voice, no exit (the index forces it on you), and little legal recourse. You get the economics and almost nothing else.

Bottom line for an ordinary holder

If you hold a Nasdaq-100 fund, you'll own SpaceX automatically and can't exclude it — that's the real, unfixable catch. The silver lining: the much-maligned 3× cap is the very thing that limits your exposure to ~4–5% instead of 10%+, and Musk staying locked up removes the biggest early seller.

The SpaceX IPO video, fact-checked

Most of the numbers are right. The framing of the one mechanism the whole thesis hangs on — the Nasdaq weighting rule — is pointed the wrong way. Here's the separation of signal from theatre.

The video's factual spine is largely accurate: the three-business structure, Starlink's economics, the consolidated loss, the fast-entry rule, the float-minimum removal, and the 30% retail allocation all check out against primary sources.

The genuinely sound takeaway survives all of this: passive mechanics convert index membership into price-insensitive, mandatory buying, and the offering is structured so insiders sell into that demand. That part is not hype.

02 — THE NASDAQ MACHINERYThe part the whole video hangs on

Three rule changes, effective May 1 2026 (announced March 30, after a public Feb consultation). Two are accurately described. One is described backwards.

The thesis is: Nasdaq rewrote the Nasdaq-100 rulebook so an unprofitable, low-float mega-cap can be force-fed into every index fund. The index matters because 200+ products tracking ~$600B in assets must buy whatever the index holds. Get in, and demand is automatic. That structural claim is sound. The question is which rule does the work.

RULE 1

Fast entry — 15 trading days

Accurate

Old path: a new listing waited through a seasoning period and was typically only added at the December annual reconstitution. New path: eligibility is checked as early as the 7th trading day and inclusion can happen ~15 trading days after IPO.

The video undersells its own case. It says the old wait was "three months." The realistic wait was up to ~12 months (you had to catch the annual rebalance). The compression is more dramatic than stated — but the video also drops the gate: fast entry requires ranking in the top ~40 constituents (~$100B). Irrelevant for a $1.75T company, but it means this isn't open to "any IPO."

RULE 2

Minimum free-float requirement removed

Accurate

A 10% minimum float rule — only introduced in June 2024 — would have disqualified SpaceX outright at a 3–5% float. The May 2026 update eliminates the minimum. So "it would have been an automatic disqualification six months ago" is literally true.

RULE 3

The "3× multiplier"

Described backwards

The video: ETFs are "legally required to treat SpaceX's float as if it were 3× larger… manufactured demand." This points the mechanism the wrong way.

What the rule actually does: securities with a free float below ~33.3% (the video says 20% — it mixed the draft's 20%/5× with the final 33.3%/3×) are weighted down, capped at 3× their float. Nasdaq's stated purpose is to align index weight with tradable float — i.e. to reduce the weight a thin-float giant would otherwise command, not inflate it.

The defensible kernel: relative to its actual 4–5% tradable float, a 3× cap still lets the index assign weight as if ~12–15% were floating. So ETFs do end up holding more than the free float can liquidly support — which is a real liquidity / price-impact problem. But that's the opposite framing from "the multiplier manufactures extra demand." It's a brake the video sells as an accelerator.

The rule wasn't "quietly" slipped in — it went through a published consultation. The fair worry isn't secrecy; it's that the consulted parties are the same asset managers who benefit.

03 — THE THESIS THAT SURVIVESWhy "your 401(k) is the exit" isn't just a slogan

Strip the loose numbers and the inverted multiplier, and a coherent mechanism remains. Insiders and early VCs — Founders Fund, a16z, the Saudi PIF and others — hold positions at a cost basis that is a tiny fraction of $1.75T. For them the IPO is a finish line, not a starting gun.

The structure routes their exit through buyers who can't say no. Passive funds buy on inclusion regardless of price — that's the whole point of indexing. Fast entry compresses the price-discovery window before that mandatory bid arrives. The 30% retail tranche adds a second pool of price-insensitive, sentiment-driven demand. Buyers are pulled in early; insider lock-ups release later. That sequencing — forced buyers early, sellers unlocked late — is the actual engine, and the video describes it correctly.

The irony it lands is real: the convenience of passive investing (set-and-forget, low fees) is the same machinery that buys whatever squeezes into the index, without asking you. You can agree with that and still notice the video reached for the wrong rule to dramatize it.

05 — IS THERE ENOUGH MONEY FOR THREE?SpaceX, OpenAI and Anthropic, same window

Your instinct is half-right. There are two separate "money" questions, and they behave in opposite directions.

The fear — "everything's already invested, so the third IPO gets no demand" — treats the market as a closed jar. It isn't. Capital rotates, new savings flow in monthly, sovereign funds and foreign buyers participate, and leverage exists. But within a tight clustered window, the marginal pool of allocation appetite is finite, so the effect is real — just softer than "no demand." Split it into the two channels:

CHANNEL A

The primary raise (discretionary money)

Crowdable

SpaceX wants to raise ~$75B. If OpenAI and Anthropic each chase tens of billions in the same months, they compete for the same institutional checkbooks and the same retail enthusiasm. This is genuine crowding-out: the third or weakest deal doesn't get "zero demand," it gets a thinner book and softer pricing. Demand here is elastic, not fixed — but it has a ceiling per quarter.

CHANNEL B

Index flows (forced money)

Funded by selling incumbents

This is the part you intuited, and it's the more important one. The Nasdaq-100 is cap-weighted, and index funds hold almost no cash — they're fully invested by design. So when SpaceX enters, a QQQ-type fund can't buy it with idle money. It funds the purchase by selling a slice of every existing constituent pro-rata — Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, the lot.

So yes: index inclusion rotates money out of the existing Nasdaq-100 and into the newcomers. With ~$600B tracking the index and a capped ~4–5% weight, that's roughly $15–30B of forced rotation per name — sold from incumbents, bought into SpaceX. Three entrants compound the drain on everyone already in the index.

The crucial detail that resolves your either/or: the fast-entry rule lets the index temporarily exceed 100 names rather than kicking one out for each newcomer. So it isn't "one in, one out" — it's dilution of everyone's weight. And because each newcomer's forced bid is triggered by its own inclusion, the third IPO still gets its mandatory passive bid — it does not get starved by the first two. What gets squeezed is (a) discretionary demand in the primary market, and (b) the incumbent mega-caps that get trimmed to fund all three. The passive bid isn't a shared finite pool per name; the rotation cost is borne by the rest of the index.

Each of the three gets its own forced buyer. The bill is paid by Apple, Microsoft and Nvidia holders — quietly, through rebalancing.

What to track to formulate the trade

The whole setup is governed by one ratio — forced buying ÷ tradable float. These are the inputs that move it, and the dislocations to watch (mechanics, not advice — you run your own book):

The disclosed float and the announced index weight

The single biggest unknown. A larger float (the staggered unlocks widen it fast) means a higher index weight and more forced buying — but also more supply to absorb it. Watch the prospectus float figure and Nasdaq's announced weight; that pins the dollar demand.

The inclusion timeline

Eligibility checked ~day 7; addition ~15 trading days post-IPO (≈ July 6 for a June 12 listing). The rebalance date is the mechanical-demand spike. It's fully telegraphed, so front-running tends to pull the move forward — the pop may be largely in by inclusion day.

The unlock calendar + the 30%-above-offer trigger

From section 07: a price >30% over offer releases extra insider supply. So a forced-buying pop can trigger the very selling that caps it. The 70/90/105/120/135-day tranches and the post-Q2/Q3 unlocks are scheduled supply waves — map them against expected passive inflows.

Dealer / options positioning into the rebalance

Low float + mechanical buying + heavy options interest is the textbook setup where dealer hedging flows dictate the path. Gamma positioning into the inclusion date is where the short-term asymmetry lives.

The low-ADV tail — only if weight resolves high

If SpaceX's weight lands near the full-cap ceiling, the uniform % sell becomes a meaningful fraction of daily volume for the least-liquid Nasdaq-100 names (not the mega-caps). That's the only place the incumbent sell creates a tradable mechanical dip — a mean-reversion candidate. At the realistic ~0.8% weight it's muted.

This is the most-telegraphed rebalance in years. Everyone can see it, which compresses the edge and can invert the obvious trade — the "buy the forced-buying" move may already be priced. Mechanics described here are not investment advice; size and structure are your call.

07 — THE UNLOCK, SIMULATEDWhat the lock-up actually is, and what happens when they sell

Forward-looking scenario analysis, not certainty — but anchored to SpaceX's real, filed lock-up terms, which are stranger than the hypothetical.

First, the correction: there's no one-year cap and no standard 180-day cliff. The S-1 lays out a staggered, earnings- and price-triggered release explicitly engineered to widen the float fast — which feeds straight into the index-weight machinery from section 02. Critically, no insiders cash out at the listing itself; the IPO sells only new (primary) shares from the company. Insiders exit later, on this schedule:

Listing day

Friends & family carve-out — no lock-up

Directed-share / "friends and family" recipients are exempt from lock-up entirely and can sell immediately. Everyone else is restricted.

Remainder released — but Musk stays locked

Whatever's left comes off restriction at 180 days. Musk is excluded from the accelerated schedule and holds the full period; 60%+ of pre-IPO shares (including his) stay under extended lock-up. The single largest seller is removed from the early window — a real stabiliser.

The simulation: what happens when big holders sell

Assume a June listing, fast-entry index inclusion ~15 trading days later, a 3–5% float, and the staggered unlocks above. Three paths:

SCENARIO A

Orderly grind

Base case

Forced passive buying + retail loyalty drive an early pop. Staggered releases meter supply; Musk's full lock caps the overhang. Price holds, then drifts as float expands. This is the historical mega-IPO pattern: pop ~15–20%, then give it back over 6–12 months as supply meets a fixed-ish passive bid.

SCENARIO B

Pop, trigger, dump

Tail risk

Index inclusion + retail FOMO push the stock >30% above offer → that fires the extra 10% unlock on top of the 20% post-Q2 release. ~30% of insider shares hit a 3–5% float at once — analysts have floated this as potentially the largest single-day insider selling event in market history. Sharp reversal; the late retail and passive buyers own the top. The pop causes the supply that breaks it.

SCENARIO C

Soft tape

If macro turns

If rates rise, sentiment sours, or the OpenAI/Anthropic deals drain demand (section 05), the mandatory passive bid still happens — but clears at a lower price. Insiders still unlock on schedule. Result: a faster, quieter slide, with passive holders marked down and unable to opt out.

Where the knife-edge sits: before, at, or after the retirement bid

The forced retirement money doesn't arrive at listing — it arrives at index inclusion, ~15 trading days later (~July 7). There's a ~3-week gap, and the pop is built to crest right into it.

Jun 12 — listing

Trading opens at $135

Jun 12 → ~Jul 7 — the run-up

Because inclusion is dated and certain, traders buy ahead of the forced bid specifically to sell into it. Front-running + retail FOMO lift the price into inclusion. The pop is here — before the funds buy.

The drawdown

That sequence is "your 401(k) is the exit liquidity" stated mechanically: the pop is sold to the retirement bid. Three caveats keep it honest — if front-running is aggressive the move can be largely done before inclusion and "sell the news" starts right at the buy; if demand is soft the forced bid acts as a floor not a peak, and the drawdown comes later via lock-ups; and there are arguably two pops (day-one open, and the inclusion run-up) that can blur together. The shape is high-confidence; whether the knife-edge sits just before, at, or just after July 7 is the genuinely uncertain part — and where the money is made or lost.

But it ramps. The cap equals 3× float, so as lock-ups release and floats widen over the following 6–18 months, each name's allowed weight climbs toward full cap — roughly 6–7× as float grows from ~5% to ~33%. So this isn't one shock: it's three staggered inclusions, each trailed by a months-long weight ramp. The forced rotation out of incumbents escalates through 2026–27 rather than resolving on three rebalance days.

The deeper risk is correlation, not size. The Nasdaq-100 already leans hard on a single AI-capex factor (Nvidia, Broadcom, Microsoft). Stacking SpaceX (which now contains xAI), OpenAI and Anthropic adds three more frontier-AI bets — richly valued, two deeply loss-making — into the same index, all tied to one narrative. If the AI-capex thesis wobbles, all three deflate together, inside everyone's "diversified" index fund. Owning the index becomes a more concentrated AI wager, not a hedge against one.

And you may already own them — twice. The index holds these labs indirectly today: Microsoft owns ~27% of OpenAI; Amazon and Alphabet hold large Anthropic stakes; SpaceX owns xAI. So incumbents trimmed in the rebalance may simultaneously be marked up on their stakes — a partial offset — while index holders end up with double exposure (Anthropic directly via the index, and again through Amazon and Alphabet).

What to track across the three

Three rebalance dates, not one

SpaceX (~Jun–Jul), OpenAI (~Sep–Oct), Anthropic (~Oct–Nov) each trigger a separate mechanical-demand spike. Map them — and the combined NDX weight trajectory, which climbs past ~1.5% as floats expand.

Primary-market fatigue and the first-mover race

$175B+ of issuance in months tests finite discretionary demand. Watch whether the last to price books soft (order book, pricing vs. range). OpenAI and Anthropic are racing to list first to capture enthusiasm — the laggard likely gets a thinner reception.

AI-factor concentration in QQQ

Track the correlation of the three newcomers with the existing AI cluster. The cleaner read on "is this an AI bubble in the index" is the combined weight of AI-narrative names, not any single ticker.

Cross-holding marks

Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet carry stakes that get re-marked at each listing. Their rebalance trim may be offset by stake mark-ups — a pair-trade nuance, and a warning against double-counting your own exposure.

Caveat: sequencing, floats, valuations — and whether all three even list — are unsettled. Anthropic has said publicly it hasn't decided if it will go public; OpenAI's public-benefit-corp structure is novel and could slip the timeline. The figures here assume ~$1T IPO targets and ~5% floats; the live prospectuses will move them. Mechanics, not investment advice.

09 — THE FLOAT, AND THE ROADS NOT TAKENWhat a 5% float really is — and how else it could have been done

The thin float is the amplifier behind Sections 06–07. Here's the mechanism, and the menu of alternatives that shows it was a choice, not a necessity.

What "float" actually is. A company's shares split into two buckets: stock locked in insiders' hands, and stock free to trade in public hands — the free float. It is not the same as shares outstanding, and it's the number that governs liquidity, index weight, and price behaviour, because only floating shares can be bought and sold day to day. SpaceX has ~13.1 billion shares total; ~555.6 million go to the public — so the tradable bucket is a sliver, and a sliver moves on small pressure.

How that compares to a normal IPO. A typical offering floats 10–20%. For SpaceX, matching that would mean selling ~1.31B shares ($177B) to ~2.62B shares ($354B). But $75B is already the largest IPO in history (Aramco's prior record was ~$29B), so a "normal" percentage would be 6–12× the all-time record — unabsorbable. That's the pattern for giants: Aramco floated just 1.5%, Meta ~15%, Alibaba ~12%. The bigger the company, the smaller the percentage it can realistically sell.

Constraint vs. choice. Two facts feel like one. You genuinely cannot float 15% of a $1.75T company — that ceiling is a real constraint. But the float is also structured for scarcity: all-primary, fixed price, minimum viable size, staggered release, paired with forced index demand. That part is a choice. The constraint caps how much he can sell; it dictates nothing else, and they sat well below even that ceiling, then optimised the rest.

The decoupling that dissolves the "no choice" story. Low float is not required for control — the dual-class shares (Section 10) already lock that in. Musk keeps ~85% of the vote regardless of how much economics he sells, so he could have floated two or three times more and still controlled the company outright. "Thin float = keeping control" is a red herring; the super-voting shares do that job. The float's remaining purpose is scarcity, price support, and minimal dilution — not control.

So what were the roads not taken?

OPTION A

Include secondary shares

This is an all-primary deal — every share is newly issued, no insider sells at the IPO. Adding secondary shares (early VCs selling into the offering) would counterintuitively produce a healthier float: it pre-clears insider supply at the disclosed offer price instead of dumping it post-lockup into a thin market, widening the tradable base and shrinking the overhang. Cost: it looks like insiders cashing out, and it dilutes the scarcity premium.

OPTION B

Auction or direct listing

They set a fixed $135 price. Google used a Dutch auction to let demand set the price and kill the underwriting pop; Spotify and Coinbase used direct listings that float existing shares with no traditional lock-up and market-set pricing — both produce a deeper float and discovered price. Catch: a pure direct listing raises no capital, and SpaceX needs the $75B — but an auction, or a direct-listing-with-raise, were on the table.

OPTION C

Don't rush the index

The quietest lever, maybe the most important. A thin float is only a reliable pump if a price-insensitive forced buyer is arranged in advance — which fast-entry does. Absent that rule, a tiny float is mostly a liability: illiquid, volatile, no guaranteed bid. List normally, let it season, let the float widen, then enter the index at a discovered price — and the squeeze dynamic largely disappears.

The thin float and the index-rule change are complements, not separate decisions. Scarcity is only safe and profitable because a forced buyer was arranged first. One without the other is just risk.

The honest other side. A smaller float isn't purely cynical: every share dilutes existing holders, so raising only what you need is discipline; flooding supply can botch a launch (Facebook, 2012); a large insider secondary signals "the smart money is leaving." Real reasons. But notice the asymmetry — the benefits (price support, low dilution, the option to sell higher later) accrue to the company and insiders, while the costs (volatility, squeeze, pop-and-dump exposure, no liquidity cushion) land on public holders, many of whom never chose to buy because the index put it in their fund.

So is it the only way? No. The size constraint forces a small percentage — full-percentage floats were genuinely impossible. Everything past that — primary-only, fixed price, minimum float, staggered release, rushed inclusion — is an optimisation stack tilted toward maximum price and minimum dilution for the issuer. A cleaner path existed: float more economics (control was never at risk), use an auction or partial direct listing for honest price discovery, include some secondary to deepen the float, let index inclusion happen on a normal timeline. Comparable capital, a more stable stock, a better deal for the public — it just leaves the scarcity premium on the table and looks less triumphant on day one.

10 — CONTROL & RECOURSEWhat public shareholders actually get: the economics, and almost nothing else

The governance pillar the rest of the document implies — legal in every individual part, engineered as a whole.

The dual-class mechanism. Two share classes: Class A (public) at one vote, Class B (insiders) at ten. That 10:1 ratio decouples ownership from control. Per the S-1, Musk holds 12.3% of Class A and 93.6% of Class B → ~85% of total voting power on ~42% of the equity (filings range ~79–85%). A minority economic stake becomes a near-total grip on every shareholder vote. Public buyers get the price exposure and, in the filing's own logic, no practical ability to influence anything.

Where it sits on the spectrum. Dual-class is common — Meta and Alphabet do it — and is pitched as protecting a founder's long-term vision from activist pressure. SpaceX's version is at the harsh end: CalPERS and the New York State and City comptrollers jointly called it "even more restrictive than the dual-class arrangements at Meta, Alphabet, or Snap." This is the aggressive version, not the routine one.

"Controlled company" status. When one holder commands a voting majority, the exchange lets the company switch off guardrails. SpaceX is taking the exemptions, so it can skip a majority-independent board, an independent compensation committee, and an independent nominating committee. Only the audit committee survives (SEC-required). So the people who set pay, choose director nominees, and form the board majority need not be independent of the man they oversee — and Musk is simultaneously CEO, CTO and Chair on a nine-person board. The supervisor is the supervised.

The unfireable lock. Removing Musk requires a Class B vote, which Musk controls — so, as the institutional letter put it, "as a mathematical matter, he can only be fired if he votes against himself." Public owners cannot remove him, replace the board, force a sale, or win a proxy fight. The normal levers shareholders use to discipline a CEO are, by design, unreachable.

What it enables: related-party risk. No independent board means no structural check on deals between the company and its controller. The textbook example already happened pre-IPO: SpaceX bought Musk's xAI for ~$250B in all-stock, on terms set by boards Musk controls — folding a loss-making AI unit into what the public is now buying. There is no built-in mechanism to block the next such deal.

What the public holder lacks

  • No voice — the share class carries votes that can never be decisive.
  • No exit — via index inclusion, a passive holder owns SpaceX whether they object to the governance or not.
  • No recourse — a mandatory-arbitration clause blocks class actions in federal court; even a CalPERS-scale holder can hardly sue.

Who is refusing it

  • AkademikerPension (Denmark) blacklisted SpaceX for "catastrophic governance" and overvaluation.
  • CalPERS + NY State & City comptrollers filed a formal objection itemising the founder-serving provisions.
  • Serious institutional money is declining on governance grounds — even as the index forces it onto everyone else.

No voice, no exit, no recourse — every piece legal and disclosed, assembled to leave public owners the downside and none of the levers.

The accurate label isn't "illegal" or even quite "grey area." It's legal-but-engineered: no rule requires a minimum IPO float; dual-class voting, controlled-company exemptions and mandatory arbitration are all legal, disclosed, and individually defensible; and the index rule passed a public consultation. The controversy is who shaped the rules and who can't escape them — not whether anyone broke one. "Bend, don't break."
