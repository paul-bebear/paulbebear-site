    How it works

    A light‑year is a distance

    A is how far light travels in one year: about 9.46 trillion kilometres. Light is the fastest thing there is, but space is so big that even light takes years to cross the gap between stars.

    So every star is a view of the past

    When you look at a star 30 light‑years away, you see light that left it 30 years ago. If you are 30, that light set off around when you were born and has been travelling ever since. That's your : an imaginary bubble around Earth, as many light‑years across in radius as you are years old, growing by one light‑year every birthday. It works both ways, too: radio and TV broadcast the week you were born are only now washing over those same stars.

    How do we know how far away a star is?

    With . Hold a finger up and close one eye, then the other: the finger jumps against the background. Nearby stars do the same as Earth swings around the Sun. Measure the jump and simple geometry gives the distance. The jumps are tiny, measured in : Alpha Centauri, the nearest bright star, shifts by less than a thousandth of a degree.

    Six months apart, Earth sees a nearby star against different background stars. The angle p is its parallax. Half the angle, double the distance.

    Two space telescopes did the measuring

    Most distances here come from , a European space telescope that measured nearly two billion stars. The brightest stars dazzle Gaia's cameras, so for many of them we use the older mission instead. Each star's details say which one it came from.

    Every distance has an error bar

    Each parallax comes with an . We use a range that should contain the true distance about 95 times in 100. For most stars that range is a few weeks to a few months of light travel. For some bright stars and double stars it is years. A star counts as yours when that range overlaps your light shell, which we take as your age give or take six months.

    Why there's no exact date

    Even the best distances are uncertain by hours or days of light travel, and most by weeks or more. Stars also move: a typical star's distance from us changes by a day or two of light travel over a human lifetime, so the gap shifts a little while the light is on its way. And Gaia's measurements carry a small systematic offset that is still being studied. So we never give a light‑birthday to the day. The narrowest we go is "around March 2031", and many stars get a range of years.

    And astrology?

    The is where this page started, and it is a good reason to look up. But the stars here don't know when you were born. What's true is stranger: some of the light landing on your eyes tonight set off from those stars at the very start of your life.

    Stars. Every star in Stellarium's hip_gaia3 catalogue (levels 0 to 2) with a parallax of at least 27.18 milliarcseconds, which is 120 light‑years or closer: 3,795 stars, down to about magnitude 9 for most of the sky, plus fainter Hipparcos stars and a few fainter Gaia stars. The faintest red dwarfs and most white dwarfs nearby are not in this list, so some real stars on your shell are missing. Also drawn: the stars of the 13 zodiac figures that lie farther away, and, with "Farther stars", every star brighter than magnitude 4.5 beyond 120 light‑years, for orientation.

    Distances. Distance in light‑years = 3,261.56 ÷ parallax in milliarcseconds. The range is the parallax plus or minus two standard errors (about 95%), with a floor of 0.02 milliarcseconds. Each star's source is labelled Gaia DR3 when its parallax matches Gaia DR3 as listed in AT-HYG, Hipparcos when it matches the 2007 Hipparcos reduction or the star has no Gaia entry, and "Gaia or Hipparcos" when Stellarium's value matches neither listing exactly. Alpha Centauri A and B use Kervella et al. (2016). Positions are for 2016; the sky map ignores the small shifts since.

    Dates. A light‑birthday is your birth date plus the distance in Julian years (365.25 days, the year used to define the light‑year). Its range comes from the distance range. Dates shown are never more precise than a month. Your light shell is your age now, give or take half a year, and a star counts as yours when its distance range overlaps it. Stars whose range is wider than plus or minus five light‑years (56 of them, mostly double stars) are never counted as anyone's: they are too uncertain to call. Star signs use the usual astrological dates; the constellation behind the Sun uses approximate dates from the IAU constellation boundaries, which drift by about a day every 70 years.
