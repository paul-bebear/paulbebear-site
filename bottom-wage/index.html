How it's measured

The worker

One adult, no children, working 40 hours a week all year (2,080 hours). Living alone in a modest one-bedroom.

The two wages

Legal minimum: the 2026 floor that applies inside the city. Real bottom wage: what the lowest-paid tenth of jobs in the metro area pay (10th percentile, all occupations).

Costs

Rent is HUD's Fair Market Rent for a one-bedroom, utilities included. Transport is a transit pass in New York City and typical car and transit costs everywhere else.

Taxes

2026 rules: federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare, state and city income taxes, Denver's head tax, and the federal and state Earned Income Tax Credits (assumes the worker is 25 or older).

Hours needed = (monthly rent + monthly transport) / after-tax hourly pay. Fewer hours means more of the month left for everything else.

What this leaves out

  • The 10th percentile is not "the minimum-wage worker". It is the pay of the lowest-paid tenth of jobs, and the mix of jobs differs by city. It also excludes the self-employed.
  • The legal-minimum view is mostly hypothetical in some places. Almost nobody in Salt Lake City, Houston or Jackson earns $7.25; the market sets entry pay higher.
  • City rules, metro data. Minimum wages are for the city itself, but wages, rents and transport cover the metro area. The New York numbers include New Jersey suburbs (minimum $15.92). That is also why the real bottom wage sits below the city minimum in Seattle, Denver and Miami: their metros include suburbs with lower floors, and the wage data predate the 2026 raises.
  • Transport is the biggest assumption. Car costs are typical spending from the MIT Living Wage Calculator ($640 to $830 a month). A worker with a paid-off used car pays less; a Seattle or Denver worker on transit would need fewer hours. The headline result is robust to it: on the real bottom wage, Salt Lake City stays ahead of New York unless driving there costs more than $1,170 a month.
  • Rents are for people moving in now. Long-time tenants, including about a million New York rent-stabilized households, pay less. Most low earners also share rather than live alone; splitting a two-bedroom roughly halves the rent.
  • Health coverage is left out, and it may matter more than taxes. A full-time $7.25 earner makes about $15,100 a year, below the poverty line. In Utah, which expanded Medicaid, that means free coverage. In Texas and Mississippi, which did not, it means falling in the gap: too poor for marketplace subsidies, not eligible for Medicaid. Coverage is hard to price and federal rules are changing in 2026 and 2027, so it is not in the numbers.
  • Food, phone and everything else are left out on purpose. They vary less between cities than rent does, and pricing them adds assumptions.
  • Tipped workers are excluded. Their legal minimums are lower ($2.13 an hour in Utah, Texas and Mississippi).
  • The years don't line up. Minimums are 2026, wages are May 2025, rents are fiscal year 2026. Wages have grown since May 2025, which slightly flatters the real-bottom-wage view.
  • This is about what a wage buys, not jobs. It says nothing about whether higher minimums cost jobs, or about the career ladder a big city offers.

Rent. NLIHC, Out of Reach 2026: one-bedroom Fair Market Rent by metro area, from HUD's fiscal year 2026 Fair Market Rents.

Transport. NYC: MTA weekly fare cap of $35 from January 2026, times 52 weeks / 12. Other cities: MIT Living Wage Calculator, 1 adult with no children, transportation line, data updated February 2026.
