Part 2

In depth

1. The test: is it an asset?

Spending becomes capex only if it creates an asset: a resource the company controls, that is expected to bring future economic benefits, and whose cost can be measured reliably. For intangibles like software, IAS 38 adds that it must be identifiable (separable, or arising from a contract or legal right).

Control is the word people skip. It means the power to get the benefits and to stop others getting them, usually through a legal right. You do not need to own the IP: a licence you control is an asset. A subscription you lose the day you stop paying is a service, so it is opex.

Importance, size or "strategic" labels do not make something capex. Only the asset test does.

2. Depreciation and amortisation

Same mechanism, two names. Depreciation spreads the cost of physical assets (cables, transformers, servers). Amortisation does the same for intangibles (software, licences, patents). Together: D&A.

Straight-line is the default:

Annual D&A = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life
  • Useful life is an estimate of how long the asset produces value, reviewed each year. A shorter life means faster cost recognition.
  • Residual value is what it is worth at the end. For software it is usually zero.
  • Other methods exist (declining balance, units of production) when benefits are front-loaded or usage-driven.
  • Net book value = cost minus accumulated D&A. It is what the balance sheet shows.

4. Why managers like capitalising: EBITDA

The P&L runs top to bottom: revenue, minus operating costs, gives EBITDA; minus D&A gives EBIT; minus interest and tax gives net profit.

Opex sits above EBITDA. D&A sits below it. So capitalising moves cost out of EBITDA entirely, and investors, lenders and bonus schemes often watch EBITDA. It also moves the cash out of operating cash flow into investing cash flow, so "operating cash flow" looks better too.

Total profit over the asset's life is identical either way. Only the timing and the line it sits on change.

5. Regulated grid operators: where the money is

A regulated distribution operator cannot set its own prices. The regulator sets allowed revenue, roughly:

Opex allowance + Depreciation + RAB × WACC
  • RAB (regulated asset base): the regulator's version of the balance sheet. Capitalised spend enters it.
  • Depreciation pays the investment back over its regulatory life: the return of capital.
  • RAB × WACC pays for the money tied up: the return on capital. Spain sets 6.58% for 2026 to 2031.
  • Opex is recovered at best, often late or cut by efficiency targets. It earns no return. In Germany it is fixed from a base year, so new opex in another year waits for the next reset.

That asymmetry is the capex bias: capitalised spend makes money, expensed spend does not. Regulators fight it with TOTEX (treat all spending alike) or, in Italy, a fixed share of all spending capitalised regardless of its label.

The WACC is not pure margin. It compensates lenders and shareholders. The operator's real gain is the gap between the allowed rate and what its capital actually costs.

6. Things that catch people out

Upkeep is never capex

Maintaining an asset you already have is opex. Only spending that adds new capability can be added to the asset, and IAS 38 says that happens "only rarely".

No relabelling later

Once a cost has been expensed it can never be capitalised retroactively (IAS 38 para. 71).

Prepayment is not capex

Paying in advance for a service creates a short-term asset that unwinds as the service arrives. It never enters D&A.

Internal labour can be capitalised

Staff time spent building an asset can go on the balance sheet, which is one reason companies build in-house instead of buying SaaS.

Impairment

If an asset loses its value early, the remaining book value is written off in one hit. The risk of capitalising.

Three rulebooks

Statutory accounts, tax and the regulator can each use different lives. In Germany the regulator fixes software at 3 to 5 years.

Commitments stay off the balance sheet

A signed but unperformed contract is not a liability, unless it turns loss-making (onerous). Then a provision is booked.

EBITDA is not a standard

IFRS does not define EBITDA. Each company computes its own, so compare definitions before comparing numbers.

7. Glossary

Capex
Capital expenditure. Spending that creates an asset.
Opex
Operating expenditure. Spending consumed in the period.
D&A
Depreciation and amortisation: the yearly slice of capex cost in the P&L.
Net book value
Cost minus accumulated D&A.
Useful life
The estimated period an asset produces value.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation.
RAB
Regulated asset base: capital the regulator lets earn a return.
WACC
Weighted average cost of capital: the allowed rate of return on the RAB.
TOTEX
Total expenditure: regulating opex and capex together to remove the bias.
Onerous contract
One whose unavoidable costs exceed the benefits expected from it.

8. Check yourself

A company buys a perpetual software licence for €1.2m and expects to use it for 4 years. What happens to year-1 EBITDA and net profit?

EBITDA: no change, because the licence is capitalised and amortisation sits below EBITDA. Net profit: down €300k (1.2m ÷ 4), before tax. Cash: down €1.2m in investing cash flow.

A German grid operator pays €200k a year for reserved support capacity. Capex or opex, and how is it treated in a year that is not the base year?

Opex: it buys a service consumed over time, not an asset. Outside the base year it is not in the allowance, so the operator carries it until the next base-year reset. Bought in the base year itself, it is built into the following period's allowance.

Why is a 6.58% WACC not 6.58% profit for the operator?

Because it pays for the capital tied up: interest to lenders and a return to shareholders. The operator only gains where the allowed rate exceeds its real cost of capital. It is also paid on a shrinking balance, so over a 3-year life it adds up to about 13% of the original cost, not 6.58% a year on the full amount.
